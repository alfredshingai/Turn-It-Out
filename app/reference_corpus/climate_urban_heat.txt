Cities are warming faster than the countryside around them, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island effect. Dark asphalt and concrete absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly at night, which is why city centers can remain several degrees hotter than surrounding suburbs after sunset. The consequences fall unevenly. Neighborhoods with fewer trees and parks, often the poorest districts, routinely record the highest temperatures, and heat-related hospitalizations cluster in exactly those areas during summer heat waves. Researchers studying the urban heat island effect have found that the difference between the hottest and coolest neighborhoods within a single city can exceed ten degrees Fahrenheit on extreme days.

Mitigation strategies are well understood even if implementation lags. Reflective roofing, permeable pavement, and expanded tree canopy each lower surface temperatures measurably. Urban forestry programs deliver additional benefits beyond cooling: street trees filter stormwater, reduce noise, and are consistently associated with improved self-reported mental health. The central challenge is therefore not technical but political, since the neighborhoods that need cooling most are typically the ones with the least municipal investment. Effective policy requires pairing capital funding with community planning so that greening projects raise resilience without accelerating displacement of the residents they are meant to protect.
