The relationship between social media use and adolescent mental health has become one of the most contested questions in modern psychology. Between 2010 and 2015, as smartphones and image-based platforms reached near saturation among teenagers in the United States, rates of reported loneliness, anxiety, and depressive symptoms climbed simultaneously. Critics of the technology point to this timing as evidence of causation, while others note that similar trends appear in countries with very different platform cultures, which complicates any single-cause story.

Evidence from longitudinal studies suggests the picture is genuinely mixed. Effects measured at the population level tend to be small, but they are not distributed evenly: for a minority of heavy users, particularly adolescent girls engaged in appearance-focused platforms, associations with body dissatisfaction and disordered sleep are consistently stronger. Displacement of sleep and in-person interaction appears to be a more reliable mechanism than the content viewed. Policies now spreading through schools, from phone-free classrooms to age verification debates, are effectively large-scale experiments, and their results over the next decade will do more to settle the argument than any single study can.
