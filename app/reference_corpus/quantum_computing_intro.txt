A classical computer stores information in bits, each of which is either zero or one. A quantum computer uses quantum bits, or qubits, which exploit two uniquely quantum phenomena: superposition and entanglement. A qubit can exist in a combination of zero and one at the same time, and measuring it collapses that combination into a definite value. When qubits become entangled, the state of one cannot be described independently of the other, allowing quantum computers to represent certain problem spaces far more compactly than classical machines can.

The promise lies in specific problem classes rather than general speedup. Shor's algorithm factors large integers exponentially faster than the best known classical methods, which threatens the RSA encryption protecting most internet traffic today. Grover's algorithm offers a quadratic speedup for unstructured search. Quantum simulation of molecules and materials may transform drug discovery and battery chemistry, because nature itself is quantum mechanical and classical computers struggle to model it exactly. The obstacles are equally concrete: qubits decohere in microseconds, error rates compound, and useful machines will require thousands of physical qubits for every reliable logical one. The field is best understood as an engineering race to suppress errors at scale, not a magic shortcut to faster everything.
